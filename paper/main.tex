\begin{abstract}
Security professionals share much of their ransomware knowledge in
conference talks and vendor webinars, but we are not aware of an open
transcript-level corpus that covers this material. We present RansKnow,
an open corpus of 1{,}128 ransomware-related YouTube video records
(1{,}034 with transcripts) from 92 cybersecurity channels, with MITRE
ATT\&CK-aligned family, tactic, tool, and platform features extracted by
regular expressions. A label-quality audit found that 9 of 41 family
aliases collide with ordinary words such as \texttt{play} and
\texttt{hive}, which inflated the most-detected family about $3.5\times$
before a context-based fix. The corpus has no independent human-annotated
test set, so baseline, calibration, and conformal results measure
agreement with the audited pipeline, not real-world accuracy. On that
basis, baselines reach macro-F1 0.90 on relevance, 0.62 on platform, 0.38
on dominant tactic, and at most 0.20 on family and tool. Split conformal
prediction reaches 0.91--0.93 coverage at a 90\% target on the multiclass
tasks. In out-of-distribution detection, softmax methods fail a sanity
check against off-topic newsgroup posts (AUROC 0.62), while Mahalanobis
distance on embeddings passes it (0.94 after controlling for length and
formatting) but scores below chance on two
setups whose out-of-distribution videos are selected for naming a family.
We release the corpus, pipeline, and experiment code. The audit method
applies to other security text datasets labeled by keywords or regular
expressions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ransomware knowledge is scattered across incident-response writeups,
conference talks, and vendor threat-intelligence reports, and little of
it is structured for NLP or machine learning. Structured resources exist,
including the MITRE ATT\&CK framework \citep{strom2018attack} and datasets
built from written threat intelligence (\Cref{sec:related}), but we are
not aware of an open transcript-level corpus covering what security
professionals say in talks, webinars, and walkthroughs.

YouTube gathers this material from incident-response vendors, security
conferences, and independent researchers, whose incentives and house
styles differ in a way no single vendor blog or threat feed replicates.
Transcription is the first obstacle: 92.7\% of this corpus needed
automatic speech recognition rather than official captions
(\Cref{sec:transcripts}), and a single misheard term can change a
jargon-based label (\Cref{sec:finding-b}).

Labeling is the second. RansKnow's labels come from a regex pipeline, and
the project has no human-annotated evaluation set held out from that
pipeline (\Cref{sec:gold-scope}). Results from \Cref{sec:baselines}
through \Cref{sec:conformal} therefore measure agreement with the audited
pipeline, not verified accuracy.

This paper makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A corpus.} RansKnow contains 1{,}128 video records, 1{,}034
  with transcripts, from 92 cybersecurity YouTube channels, with MITRE
  ATT\&CK-aligned features (\Cref{sec:construction}). The corpus and code
  are at \url{https://github.com/Henrinnes/RansKnow}.
\item \textbf{A label-quality audit.} A dictionary-driven search for
  generic-word alias collisions found that 9 of 41 family aliases collide
  with ordinary words, which inflated the most-detected family about
  $3.5\times$ before a disambiguation fix (\Cref{sec:audit},
  \Cref{tab:alias-audit}).
\item \textbf{Weak-label baselines.} We report results across five
  prediction tasks, three feature representations, and five models, with
  calibration and conformal prediction on top (\Cref{sec:baselines,sec:uncertainty,sec:conformal}).
\item \textbf{Out-of-distribution results.} These scores need no correct
  dominant-tactic label, although two of the four setups define their
  out-of-distribution populations with the regex (\Cref{sec:ood}).
\item \textbf{Counterfactual and perturbation checks.} A classifier
  trained on the fixed labels and scored on videos it did not train on
  still ranks the videos the fix removed slightly above other negatives
  (AUROC 0.58), a weak trace of the fragility the audit found
  (\Cref{sec:interpretability}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Ransomware research.} Recent surveys concentrate on ransomware
behavior and defense: \citet{beaman2021ransomware} review advances in
prevention and detection, and \citet{oz2022survey} survey ransomware
evolution, taxonomy, and defense solutions. \citet{meland2020raas} study
the ransomware-as-a-service economy through two years of observation of
darknet markets and forums. RansKnow addresses a different question: how
practitioners describe ransomware in public talks and walkthroughs, and
how reliably that description can be turned into structured data.

\textbf{Mapping threat reports to ATT\&CK.} A related line of work
extracts structured behavior from written reports. TTPDrill
\citep{husari2017ttpdrill} extracts threat actions from threat reports
and aligns them with attack patterns, rcATT \citep{legoy2020rcatt}
compares classifiers for retrieving ATT\&CK tactics and techniques from
reports and releases a tool based on the results, and
\citet{orbinato2022mapping} report an experimental study of
machine-learning classification of unstructured threat intelligence into
attack techniques. These works take written text as input. RansKnow
applies a deterministic regex method to mostly ASR transcripts and audits
the labels that method produces.

\textbf{Ransomware and cyber-threat-intelligence datasets.} RansKnow's
tactic taxonomy follows the MITRE ATT\&CK framework
\citep{strom2018attack}, and prior datasets build on that framework using
written cyber-threat-intelligence (CTI) text. MalwareTextDB
\citep{lim2017malwaretextdb} provides span-level annotations of malware
characteristics (39 APT reports, 6{,}819 sentences), and AnnoCTR
\citep{lange2024annoctr} links entities, tactics, and techniques across
400 commercial CTI reports. Both rely on human annotation of written vendor
reports and blog posts. RansKnow differs in two ways. Its source material
is spoken narration, 92.7\% of it transcribed by ASR
(\Cref{sec:transcripts}), from vendor analysts, conference presenters, and
independent researchers. Its features come from an audited regex and
keyword pipeline (\Cref{sec:audit}) rather than human annotation, which
gives weaker labels in exchange for scale and openness. The closest work on
security talks is \citet{walter2026whoshares}, who release a corpus
of 9{,}728 talks from academic and industry security conferences and map
offensive talks to ATT\&CK. They rely on titles and abstracts because video
recordings were published inconsistently and automated transcription remained
imperfect. RansKnow uses the transcripts themselves, and Finding~B
(\Cref{sec:finding-b}) reports one effect of that transcription error. We are
not aware of a comparable open, transcript-level, MITRE-aligned video corpus, although
our search was not a systematic review.

\textbf{Weak and distant supervision.} Replacing human labels with an
automated heuristic is a well-studied tradeoff.
\citet{mintz2009distant} formalized distant supervision for relation
extraction by aligning text against a knowledge base, accepting label noise
in exchange for scale, and \citet{ratner2017snorkel}'s Snorkel combines
multiple, possibly conflicting, weak labeling functions probabilistically.
The Knowledge Agent (\Cref{sec:audit}) is a single deterministic labeling
function. That makes it simpler to audit: the alias-collision analysis in
\Cref{sec:finding-a} would be harder against a black-box combination of
weak labelers. It also rules out cancelling noise through redundancy, which
motivates the scope note in \Cref{sec:gold-scope}. Label-quality research reaches similar concerns from
the model side: confident learning \citep{northcutt2021confident}
characterizes and identifies label errors in datasets, and
\citet{northcutt2021pervasive} estimate an average of 3.3\% label errors
across the test sets of 10 widely used benchmarks and show that such
errors can destabilize benchmark results. RansKnow's audit starts from
the labeling function instead, searching its alias list for collisions
with ordinary vocabulary.

\textbf{Conformal prediction and out-of-distribution detection.} Split
conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005algorithmic, lei2018distribution}
offers a distribution-free coverage guarantee (\Cref{sec:conformal}) and
has seen growing use in NLP and security as an alternative to ad hoc
confidence thresholds. \citet{angelopoulos2023gentle} survey the methods
that \Cref{sec:conformal-method} draws on. Out-of-distribution detection
(\Cref{sec:ood}) with softmax-confidence baselines
\citep{hendrycks2017baseline}, energy scores \citep{liu2020energy}, and
class-conditional embedding distance \citep{lee2018simple} is less explored
for weak-label security NLP classifiers. \Cref{sec:ood-results} shows that
confidence-based methods can fail there in a domain-specific way: a
classifier's most confident predictions coincide with the ``nothing
detected'' signature that off-topic input also produces.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:construction}

RansKnow \citep{ransknow_kaggle, ransknow_github} consists of 1{,}128 video
records from 92 cybersecurity YouTube channels. Of these, 1{,}034 have an
extracted transcript with real content (four videos are listed twice, under
two registry entries for the same organization, so the 1{,}034 transcripts
cover 1{,}030 distinct videos). The remaining 94 have no usable
transcript.\footnote{We checked this against the released
  \texttt{transcripts/} tree: no method in the fetch cascade
  (\Cref{sec:transcripts}) produced usable text for any of the 94, and none
  was excluded after fetching for another reason.}

\subsection{Channel selection}
We curated channels in two batches. Batch 1 (50 channels, \texttt{C01}--\texttt{C50})
covers established DFIR vendors, security conferences, and independent
researchers. Batch 2 (51 candidate channels, \texttt{C51}--\texttt{C101})
expanded coverage into threat-intelligence vendors, additional conferences,
and OT/ICS-security and government channels. Of the 51 candidates, 42
yielded usable transcripts, 1 was a confirmed duplicate of a Batch 1
channel, 4 had no discoverable dedicated YouTube channel, and 4 resolved to
real channels with no ransomware-keyword-matching content in their upload
history. Channel-level provenance is released with the corpus in
\path{rubrics/Dataset_Channel_Registry_Updated_50_fixed_urls.xlsx}
(Batch 1) and \path{rubrics/Dataset_Channel_Registry_Batch2.xlsx}
(Batch 2).

\subsection{Video selection}
Within each channel, we scanned recent uploads for a fixed 21-term keyword
list (\texttt{ransomware}, \texttt{extortion}, \texttt{lockbit},
\texttt{incident response}, \dots; full list in \ref{app:keywords}) matched
against title and description, with a cap of 20 videos per channel and a
minimum duration of 5 minutes to exclude short clips and trailers. This is a
convenience sample, not a random or exhaustive one. A video is in scope only
if its \emph{title or description} contains one of the 21 terms, which
conditions the corpus on each channel operator's titling and description
habits. A channel that discusses ransomware extensively but titles episodes
generically (e.g.\ by client-engagement number rather than threat name)
would be under-represented, and channels that name high-profile families
(\texttt{lockbit}, \texttt{conti}, \dots) in titles for engagement would be
over-represented. Keyword-based inclusion therefore adds a second bias on
top of channel curation (\Cref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Transcript acquisition}
\label{sec:transcripts}
We acquired transcripts through a three-stage cascade: (1) the YouTube Data
API's official captions where available; (2) \texttt{yt-dlp}'s
auto-generated captions; (3) local Whisper transcription
\citep{radford2023whisper} as a fallback. Only 74 of 1{,}034 transcripts
(7.2\%) came from official captions. Another 959 (92.7\%) are ASR output:
554 from \texttt{whisper\_base} (the smallest, least accurate Whisper
checkpoint) and 405 from a local Whisper fallback. One transcript came from
\texttt{yt-dlp} captions. This composition is Finding B of the label-quality
audit, and \Cref{sec:finding-b} reports its effect on jargon-term detection.

\section{Label-Quality Audit}
\label{sec:audit}

The Knowledge Agent pipeline (\texttt{Scripts/knowledge\_agent.py})
extracts structured features from every transcript with regex matching:
ransomware family mentions (against a maintained alias list), MITRE
ATT\&CK tactic mention counts across 10 tactics, offensive tool mentions
across 8 tools, and platform signals (Windows/Linux/ESXi). We report three
problems here because a classifier trained on these labels inherits their
bias.

\subsection{Finding A: generic-word alias collisions}
\label{sec:finding-a}
Nine of 41 family aliases collide with common English or business words
(\texttt{play}, \texttt{hive}, \texttt{cactus}, \texttt{agenda},
\texttt{clop}, \texttt{inc}, \texttt{maze}, \texttt{medusa},
\texttt{royal}). We identified them with a dictionary lookup against
\texttt{/usr/share/dict/words} and confirmed them by inspecting transcripts
(e.g.\ \texttt{hive} matching ``registry hive,'' a routine DFIR term, rather
than Hive ransomware). A windowed-context fix, which requires a
ransomware-context term within $\sim$15 tokens of the match, substantially
reduces false-positive volume but does not eliminate it: false positives
still occur where context vocabulary is dense (see
\texttt{Scripts/audit\_family\_aliases.py} for the method and
\Cref{tab:alias-audit} for before/after counts). We have not measured the
precision and recall of the fixed labels against human judgment, so
\Cref{tab:alias-audit} shows how many detections the fix removed, not how
many of the remaining detections are correct.

\subsection{Finding B: ASR-dominated transcript quality}
\label{sec:finding-b}
As noted in Section~\ref{sec:transcripts}, 92.7\% of transcripts are ASR
output rather than official captions. Aggregate detection rates across
\texttt{Transcript\_Provider} show no obvious gap, but mistranscribing rare
jargon can matter without moving an aggregate rate: a single missed
\texttt{rclone} mention changes that video's \texttt{Tool\_Rclone} feature
from 1 to 0.

The released dataset contains transcripts, not audio, so we automated the
planned audio-versus-transcript check. For a random sample of 40
\texttt{whisper\_base} videos, we re-fetched the opening 6 minutes of audio,
re-transcribed it with Whisper's \texttt{small} checkpoint as a stronger
reference, and checked every occurrence of six jargon terms
(\texttt{LockBit}, \texttt{Mimikatz}, \texttt{PsExec}, \texttt{Cobalt
Strike}, \texttt{rclone}, \texttt{BloodHound}, plus known phonetic-mangling
variants) against the stored \texttt{whisper\_base} transcript. Of the 40
videos, 35 were re-transcribed (5 audio downloads failed). Across those 35,
the six terms appeared in only 3 (video, term) instances within the sampled
opening 6 minutes, so jargon density is low even in ransomware-focused
content. The \texttt{whisper\_base} transcripts missed 1 of the 3 entirely:
\texttt{rclone} appears once in one video's reference transcript and zero
times (correct spelling or a known mangled variant) in its stored
transcript. The other two, \texttt{bloodhound} (same video) and
\texttt{cobalt strike} (18 mentions in another video), were detected
correctly. A miss rate of 1/3 from 3 instances is not a precise estimate.
The conclusion is qualitative: jargon mistranscription is an observed
failure mode of this corpus's dominant transcription method, consistent with
Section~\ref{sec:perturbation}'s simulated-ASR-noise sweep. A larger
spot-check over full video length is the natural follow-up.

\subsection{Scope: no independent ground truth}
\label{sec:gold-scope}
An earlier design of this project planned a held-out, independently
annotated evaluation subset (180 videos, stratified by channel type, year,
and current family count) to break the circularity of evaluating a
classifier against the pipeline that labeled its training data. That subset
was never annotated. Recruiting external annotators would have required
ethics approval beyond the project's existing approval for public data, and
was not undertaken. We considered a single-investigator pass and rejected it
as too close to the system under evaluation to serve as independent ground
truth.

As a result, \textbf{every quantitative result in
Section~\ref{sec:baselines} onward, apart from the population-separation
scores in Section~\ref{sec:ood} and the behavioral checks in
Section~\ref{sec:interpretability}, measures agreement
with the audited Knowledge Agent extraction pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:audit}),
not verified accuracy against human judgment.} We repeat this point where
each affected result is reported. Finding A shows that the pipeline's labels
contain error even after the disambiguation fix, and nothing downstream
corrects for it. Macro-F1 and calibration numbers in this paper describe how
well models reproduce a specific, imperfect, documented extraction process.
They are not claims about ransomware-domain understanding.
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to this limitation.

\section{Task Definitions and Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

Five prediction tasks are defined over the corpus: ransomware family
(multi-label, 18 classes after excluding 3 singleton-occurrence classes,
see \Cref{tab:tasks}), dominant MITRE tactic (multi-class, 11 classes),
platform signal (multi-class, 4 classes), offensive tool mentions
(multi-label, 7 classes after excluding \texttt{MegaNZ}, which has zero
occurrences in the corpus), and a ransomware-relevance binary screen
(derived, not a stored column: \texttt{Family\_Count > 0 OR
  Tactic\_Impact > 0}).

\subsection{Model ladder and validation protocol}
Each task is evaluated over two feature representations: TF-IDF and
mean-pooled sentence embeddings.\footnote{384-dim
  \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, chunked at $\sim$200 words per chunk and
  mean-pooled, computed in a dedicated Python environment
  (\texttt{torch}~2.5.1) because the reference implementation's default
  environment ships an older \texttt{torch} that does not satisfy
  \texttt{sentence\_transformers}' \citep{reimers2019sbert} requirements; see
  \texttt{Scripts/precompute\_embeddings.py}. The 20 Newsgroups embeddings
  and the experiments rerun for this version used Python~3.12.3 with
  \texttt{torch}~2.15.0, \texttt{sentence\_transformers}~6.2.0,
  scikit-learn~1.9.1, and LightGBM~4.7.0.} Structured Knowledge Agent
columns are added for \texttt{family} and \texttt{dominant\_tactic} only. We
compare five models (the rule-based Knowledge Agent as baseline 0, Logistic
Regression, Linear SVM, Random Forest, LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm}) under
three cross-validation protocols: stratified random $k$-fold,
\texttt{GroupKFold} by \texttt{Channel\_ID} (to detect channel-style leakage,
since a model can learn ``this is CrowdStrike's narration style'' instead of
the content), and a temporal split (train pre-2024, test 2024+, since 79\% of
the corpus is from 2024 onward).

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:baseline-results}
Per Section~\ref{sec:gold-scope}, every number in this section is
weak-label agreement: how well a model reproduces the Knowledge Agent's own
extraction. The rule-based Knowledge Agent baseline scores a tautological
macro-F1 of 1.000 on every task because it is checked against its own labels,
so \Cref{tab:results} omits it. Structured features are excluded from the
dominant-tactic comparison for a related reason: they contain the tactic
counts its label is the argmax of. That combination scores 0.813 with
LightGBM, which measures how well a tree model learns an argmax over its own
inputs, so \Cref{tab:results} reports the best representation without those
counts.

weak-label ceiling (\S\ref{sec:gold-scope}) and class sparsity (18-class

support) as the remaining candidates, neither of which this paper isolates.

\section{Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
\textbf{Scope note (\S\ref{sec:gold-scope} applies here):} calibration is
normally reported against ground truth: does an 80\%-confidence prediction
turn out correct roughly 80\% of the time? Without independent labels, the
only available target is the weak-label distribution, which changes what a
well-calibrated result means. A model perfectly calibrated against its own
training signal has learned to echo a regex confidently, which does not make
it a well-calibrated model of ransomware content. We report these results
because reliability diagrams and Expected Calibration Error against the weak
labels still answer a narrower question: does a model's confidence track its
\emph{agreement with the extraction pipeline}, or is it uniformly
overconfident regardless of agreement?

\subsection{Calibration against weak labels}
\label{sec:uncertainty-calibration}
For a multiclass task, let $\hat p(\cdot \mid x)$ be the trained model's
predicted class distribution for input $x$, and let $\hat c(x) =
\arg\max_c \hat p(c\mid x)$ with confidence $\hat p_{\max}(x) =
\max_c \hat p(c\mid x)$. Agreement with the weak label $y^{\mathrm{KA}}(x)$
(the Knowledge Agent's own extraction) is $a(x) = \mathbb{1}[\hat c(x) =
y^{\mathrm{KA}}(x)]$. For the two multi-label tasks (\texttt{family},
\texttt{tool}), whose models are independent per-class binary
classifiers (\Cref{sec:baselines}), each (sample, class) pair
$(x,k)$ is treated as its own calibration point, with confidence
$\hat p_{\max}(x,k) = \max\bigl(\hat p_k(x), 1-\hat p_k(x)\bigr)$ and
agreement against the binary weak label thresholded at $0.5$.

Reliability is summarized with $B{=}10$ equal-width confidence bins
$\{I_b\}_{b=1}^{B}$ and the standard Expected Calibration Error,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ece}
\mathrm{ECE} \;=\; \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{|S_b|}{N}
  \left\lvert \operatorname{acc}(S_b) - \operatorname{conf}(S_b) \right\rvert,
\qquad
S_b = \{x : \hat p_{\max}(x) \in I_b\},
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{acc}(S_b) = \frac{1}{|S_b|}\sum_{x \in S_b} a(x)$ and
$\operatorname{conf}(S_b) = \frac{1}{|S_b|}\sum_{x \in S_b} \hat p_{\max}(x)$.
Per the scope note above, $a(x)$ measures agreement with $y^{\mathrm{KA}}$,
not correctness, so \cref{eq:ece} is a \emph{weak-label ECE}. We also report
the confident-disagreement rate at threshold $\tau=0.9$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cdr}
\mathrm{CDR}_\tau \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N
  \mathbb{1}\!\left[\hat p_{\max}(x_i) > \tau\right]\cdot\bigl(1-a(x_i)\bigr),
\end{equation}
the closest available analogue to a ``confidently wrong'' rate without
independently verified labels.

of its best (feature, model) combination from \Cref{tab:results}.

\subsection{A free uncertainty proxy: Random Forest tree-vote entropy}
\label{sec:uncertainty-rf}
Reliability diagrams need a held-out calibration protocol; a Random
Forest offers a per-prediction uncertainty signal with no separate
calibration step. For an input $x$ and a forest of $T$ trees producing
individual (hard) predictions $t_1(x),\dots,t_T(x)$ over $K$ classes, let
$\pi_c(x) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^T \mathbb{1}[t_j(x)=c]$ be the empirical
vote share for class $c$. The normalized tree-vote entropy is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:entropy}
H(x) \;=\; -\frac{1}{\log K}\sum_{c=1}^{K} \pi_c(x)\,\log \pi_c(x)
  \;\in\; [0,1],
\end{equation}
with $H(x){=}0$ when every tree agrees and $H(x){=}1$ at a uniform split
across all $K$ classes. For the multi-label tasks, $K{=}2$ per class and
$H(x,k)$ is averaged over classes $k$ for a per-sample summary.
\Cref{alg:rf-entropy} is the exact out-of-fold procedure used to produce
\Cref{tab:calibration}'s entropy columns.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{RF tree-vote entropy, 5-fold out-of-fold}
\label{alg:rf-entropy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require dataset $\mathcal{D}$, task with label function $y(\cdot)$, feature builder $\phi$
\State $\mathcal{F} \gets$ 5 stratified folds of $\mathcal{D}$
\For{fold $(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}) \in \mathcal{F}$}
  \State fit $\phi$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$; fit Random Forest $\{t_j\}_{j=1}^T$ on $\phi(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}})$
  \For{$x \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}$}
    \State $t_j(x) \gets$ individual tree prediction, $j=1,\dots,T$ \Comment{sklearn returns internal integer-encoded classes here, not the original label strings; see note below}
    \State recover original class labels via the fitted forest's class index before comparing to $y(x)$
    \State compute $H(x)$ via \Cref{eq:entropy}; record $a(x) = \mathbb{1}[\text{forest majority vote} = y(x)]$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $\{(H(x), a(x))\}_{x \in \mathcal{D}}$, pooled across folds
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The comment in \Cref{alg:rf-entropy} records an implementation pitfall.
scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011scikit} fits each tree in a
\texttt{RandomForestClassifier} on internally label-encoded integer
targets, so \texttt{tree.predict()} returns class \emph{indices}, not the
original label strings the ensemble's own \texttt{.predict()} returns.
Comparing tree-level votes directly against string labels without mapping
back through the fitted forest's class index silently produces 0\% apparent
agreement on every multiclass task. Our initial calibration run showed
exactly that pattern across all three multiclass tasks, which is how we
identified the bug. \Cref{tab:calibration}'s entropy AUROC column shows the
proxy works once fixed: $0.68$--$0.98$ across tasks, best on \texttt{tool}
(many easy true negatives) and weakest on \texttt{platform}.

\section{Conformal Prediction}
\label{sec:conformal}
\textbf{Scope note:} split conformal prediction's distribution-free coverage
guarantee concerns exchangeability with the calibration set, not label
correctness. It holds when calibrated against weak labels because the
guarantee is that the \emph{weak} label falls in the predicted set
$(1-\alpha)$ of the time, not that the \emph{real-world} label does. Conformal
coverage against the Knowledge Agent's labels therefore validates agreement
with the extraction pipeline, not correctness.

\subsection{Method: split conformal with the LAC score}
\label{sec:conformal-method}
Split conformal prediction (\citealp{vovk2005algorithmic, lei2018distribution};
see \citealp{angelopoulos2023gentle} for a tutorial treatment) uses a
nonconformity score $s(x,y)$ that is small when $y$ is a plausible label
for $x$. This paper uses the least-ambiguous-set (LAC) score,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lac}
s(x,y) \;=\; 1 - \hat p(y \mid x),
\end{equation}
calibrated on a held-out split $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}$ of size $n$
disjoint from both the training and test data. The calibration threshold
uses the standard finite-sample correction,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qhat}
\hat q \;=\; \operatorname{Quantile}\!\left(
  \left\{s(x_i,y_i)\right\}_{i=1}^{n} ;\;
  \frac{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{n}\right),
\end{equation}
and the prediction set for a new input $x$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:predset}
C(x) \;=\; \bigl\{\, y \;:\; \hat p(y\mid x) \geq 1-\hat q \,\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Under exchangeability of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}$ and the test
point, this construction guarantees
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coverage}
\mathbb{P}\!\left[\, y_{\mathrm{test}} \in C(x_{\mathrm{test}}) \,\right]
  \;\geq\; 1-\alpha,
\end{equation}
with $\alpha{=}0.10$ (90\% target coverage) throughout. As the scope
note above states, $y_{\mathrm{test}}$ here is the weak label
$y^{\mathrm{KA}}$: \Cref{eq:coverage} is a distribution-free
guarantee about agreement with the extraction pipeline, not about
real-world correctness. For \texttt{dominant\_tactic} and
\texttt{platform}, \Cref{eq:lac,eq:qhat,eq:predset} apply directly over
the task's full class vocabulary. \texttt{family} and \texttt{tool} are
independent per-class binary classifiers (\Cref{sec:baselines}) with no single
multiclass structure, so each class $k$ gets its own binary LAC calibration
and its own $\hat q_k$, and we report a sample's per-label prediction sets
both individually and jointly (all labels simultaneously covered).

We run \Cref{alg:conformal} for $5$ repeated random $60/20/20$
train/calibration/test splits per task and average across repeats, because a
single 20\% calibration set at this sample size ($n{\approx}200$) does not
estimate $\hat q$ reliably.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Split conformal calibration and coverage evaluation}
\label{alg:conformal}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require dataset $\mathcal{D}$, task, target coverage $1-\alpha$, repeats $R$
\For{$r = 1,\dots,R$}
  \State split $\mathcal{D}$ into $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$ (60\%), $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}$ (20\%), $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}$ (20\%), stratified
  \State fit model on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$
  \State compute $\hat q$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}$ via \Cref{eq:lac,eq:qhat}
  \For{$x \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}$}
    \State build $C(x)$ via \Cref{eq:predset}; record $\mathbb{1}[y^{\mathrm{KA}}(x) \in C(x)]$ and $|C(x)|$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State pool test-set coverage indicators across all $R$ repeats
\State report marginal coverage, and coverage restricted to the Year${\geq}2026$ and Conference-channel slices
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Out-of-Distribution Detection}
\label{sec:ood}
OOD detection asks whether a model's own confidence or embedding-space
behavior differs between in-distribution and out-of-distribution input.
Scoring that needs no correct dominant-tactic label, since the question is
which population an input came from. The weak labels are still involved:
the shared classifier is trained on them, and the temporal and
leave-rare-family setups define their populations with the regex's family
list. Only the channel-type and external-corpus partitions are free of it.

\subsection{Setups and methods}
\label{sec:ood-setups}
We use four setups, each with a stated in-distribution
(ID)/out-of-distribution (OOD) split. Three come from the corpus's own
structure rather than synthetic corruption, and the fourth is an external
sanity check:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Temporal emergence.} ID: pre-2024 videos. OOD: videos from
  2024 or later mentioning a family whose first appearance in this corpus
  is 2024 or later. \Cref{alg:ood-verify}'s assertion step checks each
  family's first-occurrence year (Maze, Medusa, Black Basta, INC: 2024;
  Cactus: 2025). This is a family's first appearance in this corpus, not its
  real-world emergence.
\item \textbf{Channel-type shift.} ID: Vendor + DFIR channels. OOD:
  Conference + Independent channels.
\item \textbf{Leave-rare-family-out.} ID: videos never mentioning
  ``Cl0p'' (11 occurrences in total, unlike Conti, LockBit, Qilin, and Akira,
  each with 19 or more). OOD: the 11 Cl0p-mentioning videos.
\item \textbf{External corpus.} ID: RansKnow transcripts. OOD: 20
  Newsgroups \citep{lang1995newsweeder} (\texttt{rec.\allowbreak sport.\allowbreak baseball} + \texttt{sci.\allowbreak space},
  excluding 38 documents left empty after header, footer, and quote
  removal), included as a sanity check that the methods below can detect
  an obviously unrelated domain before trusting them on setups 1--3.
\end{enumerate}

Four detection methods use a shared \texttt{dominant\_tactic} classifier
(TF-IDF features with LightGBM, the best dominant-tactic result in
\Cref{tab:results} and a natural multiclass fit for the softmax-family methods
below). We refit it on each setup's ID-only training pool so that no OOD signal
leaks into fitting:
\begin{align}
  s_{\mathrm{MSP}}(x) &= 1 - \max_c \hat p(c\mid x)
    \label{eq:msp}\\
  s_{\mathrm{energy}}(x) &= -\log\sum_c \exp\bigl(f_c(x)\bigr)
    \label{eq:energy}\\
  s_{\mathrm{maha}}(x) &= \min_c \bigl(\phi(x)-\mu_c\bigr)^{\!\top} \Sigma^{-1} \bigl(\phi(x)-\mu_c\bigr)
    \label{eq:maha}\\
  s_{\mathrm{conf}}(x) &= 1 - \hat p\bigl(\hat c(x) \mid x\bigr) \qquad\text{(\Cref{eq:lac} at the predicted class)}
    \label{eq:conf-ood}
\end{align}
\Cref{eq:msp} is Hendrycks and Gimpel's max-softmax-probability baseline
\citep{hendrycks2017baseline}. \Cref{eq:energy} is the energy score
\citep{liu2020energy}, computed from LightGBM's raw pre-softmax margins
$f_c(x)$ (\emph{not} from $\hat p(c\mid x)$: probabilities already sum to
$1$, which makes \Cref{eq:energy} degenerate to a constant if computed
from them). \Cref{eq:maha} is the class-conditional
Gaussian Mahalanobis distance of \citet{lee2018simple}, with $\phi(x)$ the
384-dim mean-pooled sentence embedding (\Cref{sec:baselines}'s footnote)
and $\mu_c,\Sigma$ the ID-train class-conditional means and pooled
covariance, using the classifier's own \emph{predicted} classes to form
groups (consistent with \Cref{sec:gold-scope}: no independently verified
labels exist to group by instead). $\Sigma$ is a Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage
estimate, because the temporal setup has only 162 training videos in 384
dimensions and its sample covariance is singular. \Cref{eq:conf-ood} reuses
\Cref{sec:conformal}'s LAC score, evaluated at the predicted rather than true
class for OOD flagging. It is deliberately the same formula as \Cref{eq:msp},
but its classifier is refit on the 75\% of the training pool left after
holding out a calibration split, so its AUROCs differ slightly. We report it
separately for comparability with \Cref{eq:qhat}'s calibrated threshold (does
a point's own score exceed the 90\%-target $\hat q$), which is the
operationally distinct number.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{OOD setup evaluation}
\label{alg:ood-verify}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require corpus $\mathcal{D}$, ID/OOD partition rule for the setup
\State \textbf{assert} every claimed-emerging family's first-occurrence year matches the setup's stated cutoff \Comment{caught a factual error at this step; see note below}
\State split ID pool into $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$ (75\%) / $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\mathrm{ID}}$ (25\%)
\State fit \texttt{dominant\_tactic}/TF-IDF/LightGBM on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$
\State score $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\mathrm{ID}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{OOD}}$ under \Cref{eq:msp,eq:energy,eq:maha,eq:conf-ood}
\State report AUROC(ID vs.\ OOD) per method, plus the conformal exceedance-rate contrast
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The assertion in \Cref{alg:ood-verify} caught a drafting error. We first
drafted the temporal setup's family list from memory as ``Qilin, DragonForce,
Black Basta,'' before checking first-occurrence years against the corpus.
Qilin first occurs in 2019, not after 2023, and DragonForce does not appear in
this corpus, so that list would have produced a meaningless setup.
\Cref{tab:ood} uses the corrected list (Maze, Medusa, Black Basta, INC,
Cactus), and \Cref{alg:ood-verify} verifies it on every run.

\textbf{The confidence-based methods (MSP, energy, conformal) fail the
sanity check.} On \texttt{external\_corpus}, MSP reaches only 0.622

\textbf{Mahalanobis distance on sentence embeddings passes the sanity
check but inverts on the family-based setups.} It reaches 0.999 (95\%
interval 0.996--1.000) on \texttt{external\_corpus} and 0.672
(0.618--0.723) on channel-type shift, where Conference/Independent
channels differ detectably in embedding space; \Cref{sec:ood-leakage} shows
that both figures are optimistic and gives more conservative values. On temporal emergence
(0.220, 0.130--0.324) and leave-rare-family-out (0.145, 0.065--0.244) it
is below chance with intervals that exclude 0.5, so these are not noise:
OOD videos sit \emph{closer} to the class means than ID videos do. The
covariance estimate is not the cause, since the earlier pseudo-inverse
estimate gives 0.223 and 0.145. We tested two explanations. The first is
selection: every OOD video in these setups names a family, against 25\%
and 20\% of their ID test sets. Restricting ID to family-naming videos
($n{=}14$, $n{=}51$) raises the scores to 0.353 (0.191--0.532) and 0.257
(0.119--0.417), so the temporal inversion is no longer distinguishable
from chance, while the leave-rare-family inversion narrows but persists.
The second is length: mean-pooling more chunks pulls long videos toward
the class means (Spearman $\rho$ between distance and word count is
$-0.66$ and $-0.71$), and OOD videos are longer (median 6{,}288 vs.\
2{,}950 words and 6{,}480 vs.\ 5{,}036). Adjusting log distance for log
word count leaves 0.256 and 0.148, so length does not explain the
inversion by itself. The combined effect of the two remains untested, and
with $n_{\mathrm{OOD}}{=}11$ the leave-rare-family interval stays wide.
Because both populations are selected through the regex family list,
these two setups are a weak test of whether a detector notices novel
families.

\textbf{The conformal exceedance-rate contrast (\Cref{eq:qhat}'s $\hat
q$) is uninformative here by construction.} The calibrated threshold is
at least 0.997 in every setup, because the classifier assigns the weak
label almost no probability on more than a tenth of its calibration
points ($n$ between 41 and 194). The OOD-flagging score is taken at the
\emph{predicted} class (\Cref{eq:conf-ood}), so it cannot exceed $1-1/K$,
about 0.91 with 11 classes, and no point can cross the threshold.
\Cref{eq:conf-ood}'s AUROC column is the more informative number from this
method; the zero exceedance rates are in the released JSON
(\path{outputs/phase4_ood.json}).

\subsection{Leakage and confound checks}
\label{sec:ood-leakage}
We audited these results for information shared between training and
evaluation (\texttt{Scripts/phase4\_leakage\_audit.py}). No video or URL
appears in both training and evaluation in any setup, and every fitted
component (vectorizer, classifier, class means, covariance) uses training
data only. Four indirect effects were tested.

\emph{Shared channels.} The ID split is random by video, so 98--100\% of ID
test videos share a channel with training, while the OOD channels of
channel-type shift never do, which favors separation. Holding ID channels
out lowers its mean Mahalanobis AUROC over 5 seeds from 0.677 to 0.644
(range 0.608--0.688), so the 0.672 in \Cref{tab:ood} is modestly inflated
but the effect survives. The inversions also survive: temporal emergence
stays at 0.199, or 0.204 when OOD is limited to channels unseen in training,
and leave-rare-family-out at 0.124. Every Cl0p video comes from a channel
that also appears in training, so that setup cannot be held out on both sides.

\emph{Duplicates.} Eight transcript pairs exceed 0.98 cosine similarity,
including the four videos listed twice (\Cref{sec:construction}). Dropping
evaluation videos with a near-duplicate in training changes no Mahalanobis
AUROC by more than 0.004.

\emph{Unmatched family mentions.} Four of the 217 pre-2024 ID videos contain
a term matching an emerging family that the context-gated regex did not tag.
Removing them moves temporal emergence's Mahalanobis AUROC from 0.220 to
0.261, and no ID video contains a Cl0p term.

\emph{Length and formatting in the sanity check.} The stored transcripts
contain timestamp lines and have a median of 4{,}985 words, while the 20
Newsgroups posts have no timestamps and a median of 74 words, and
Mahalanobis distance falls as documents get longer. Removing timestamps from
the corpus and re-embedding it moves the sanity check only from 0.999 to
0.996. Truncating each ID test video to its first 200-word chunk as well
lowers it to 0.943 (0.930--0.955), which we take as the fairer figure. It is
conservative, because the same truncation alone separates ID videos from
their own full-length versions at AUROC 0.994. Timestamp removal moves the
other Mahalanobis AUROCs by at most 0.022. Effects of timestamps on the
TF-IDF baselines in \Cref{sec:baselines} were not separately tested.

Taken together, \Cref{sec:ood-results} does not support a general claim
that this pipeline's models reliably detect distributional shift. It
supports a narrower one: softmax-confidence methods have an identifiable
blind spot on this classifier (the ``no signal'' vs.\ ``wrong domain''
confusion above), embedding distance detects a change of domain and, more
modestly, of channel type, and neither approach shows that it notices a
video naming a family absent from training.

\section{Interpretability and Perturbation Robustness}
\label{sec:interpretability}
Unlike Sections~\ref{sec:baselines}--\ref{sec:conformal}, this section asks
about model \emph{behavior}: does it exploit shortcuts, what does it rely on,
and how much do its predictions move under semantically irrelevant edits?
These questions need no independent ground truth, so
Section~\ref{sec:gold-scope}'s scope note applies only where a model's
training labels matter.

\subsection{Confound checks}
\label{sec:confounds}
\textbf{Length.} Transcript word count correlates moderately with
\path{Tactic_Total_Mentions} (Pearson $r=0.409$) and weakly with
\texttt{Family\_Count} ($r=0.131$), and not significantly with
\path{Tool_Total_Mentions} ($r=0.043$), so the confound is not uniform across
signal types. Dividing every tactic's count for one video by that video's word
count cannot change which tactic has the highest count, because argmax is
scale-invariant per row, so the confound cannot affect how the
\texttt{Dominant\_Tactic} label was derived. The remaining question is whether
it inflates a model given those counts. Re-scoring logistic regression on
structured features with length-normalized (mentions per 1{,}000 words) instead
of raw counts gives 0.753 macro-F1 against 0.764, a 1.1-point difference, so
the model is not exploiting length. This check covers
only the structured representation, which \Cref{tab:results} excludes for
this task as circular; length is not tested for the TF-IDF model.

\textbf{Channel identity.} 486 of 1{,}034 transcripts (47\%) contain at
least one literal mention of their own channel's name. Masking those
mentions and re-scoring TF-IDF LogReg under both CV protocols leaves the
\texttt{channel\_grouped}-vs-\texttt{stratified\_random} gap unchanged
for \texttt{family} (0.028 raw $\to$ 0.031 masked) and for
\texttt{dominant\_tactic} (0.020 raw $\to$ 0.029 masked). Whatever a
model exploits when a channel is not held out, it is not the channel
literally naming itself: the leakage this split protocol exists to catch is
stylistic or topical, and name-masking does not address it.

\subsection{Counterfactual explanations}
\label{sec:counterfactual}
Finding A raises a question: does a classifier trained on the \emph{fixed}
labels still show the regex's original word-collision fragility? We trained
TF-IDF+LogReg models on the corrected \texttt{Family} labels in 5 stratified
folds and scored every video only with a model that did not train on it,
because the videos in question are labeled not-\texttt{Play} in the training
data. The test set is the 275
videos the Section~\ref{sec:finding-a} fix removed from \texttt{Play},
meaning the fix's own judgment rather than a per-video human review. Of
these, 8 (2.9\%) still reach $P(\text{Play})\geq 0.5$, with a maximum of
0.999. Their median probability is 0.005, against 0.001 for the 648 other
not-\texttt{Play} videos, and the model ranks them above those videos with
AUROC 0.58 (standard error about 0.02). That is a weak trace of the
collision, neither a clean result nor wholesale inheritance. It should be read
against a weak model: only 18.9\% of the 111
videos labeled \texttt{Play} reach 0.5, with a median of 0.019.

We then asked what drives the model's confident \texttt{Play} predictions,
using a greedy word-\emph{type} removal counterfactual search (remove the
vocabulary word whose removal most reduces $P(\text{Play})$, repeat until the
prediction flips or a budget is exhausted) on the 8 labeled \texttt{Play}
videos with the highest held-out probability (all at least 0.97).
\texttt{play} appears in 3 of 8 removal sets, and 0 of 8 cases flip on removing
``play'' alone. Every case flips within 1--8 removals, mostly generic words
rather than ransomware-specific vocabulary: the ASR filler ``uh'' appears in 5
of 8 sets, and one case flips on removing ``uh'' alone. The model's confident
predictions therefore rest on a diffuse, easily disturbed set of words rather
than one trigger word or a clear ransomware vocabulary, a finding about the
decision boundary that is separate from the word-collision question.

\subsection{Perturbation robustness}
\label{sec:perturbation}
As an untargeted counterpart to the counterfactual search, we apply two
perturbation types and measure flip rate (does the top prediction change) and
mean absolute probability drift on freshly fit TF-IDF+LogReg models.
\emph{Simulated ASR noise} (compound proper nouns split apart, e.g.\
\texttt{WannaCry}$\to$``wanna cry,'' \texttt{Mimikatz}$\to$``mimi cats,''
plus 8\% random token deletion) is applied to the 74 official-caption videos,
to simulate what those transcripts would look like after the ASR cascade that
produced the other 92.7\% of the corpus (Finding B). \emph{Synonym
substitution} (common non-jargon words swapped for synonyms) is applied to a
150-video sample.

\texttt{dominant\_tactic}, \texttt{platform}, and \texttt{relevance} are
essentially unmoved by either perturbation (0.0--0.7\% flip rate, mean
drift $<0.01$ in every case). \texttt{family} flips 20--26\% of the time
under both perturbations despite a much smaller mean drift ($\sim$0.002)
than any other task. This is the weak-model issue from \Cref{tab:results}
(\texttt{family}'s best macro-F1 is 0.129) appearing in another form: closely
bunched, low-confidence probabilities mean a negligible nudge can change which
class wins. We did not run a gradient-based adversarial attack, because no real
adversary submits transcripts to evade this classifier in production. The
practical risks are ASR transcription noise and channel-style variation, which
this sweep and the channel-grouped protocol address. Together with
Section~\ref{sec:ood}'s temporal-emergence and leave-rare-family-out setups,
these results describe how the system handles evolving, novel, and noisy input.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}
\label{sec:limitations}

The most consequential limitation of this paper is the absence of independent
ground truth. The other limitations (sampling bias, ASR noise, source rights,
dual use) are of a more familiar kind, but the ground-truth gap changes what
every quantitative claim in Sections~\ref{sec:baselines}--\ref{sec:conformal}
\emph{means}, not only how confidently it can be stated.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No independent ground truth.} This project has no
    human-annotated evaluation set independent of the Knowledge Agent
    pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:gold-scope}). Recruiting external annotators
    would have required ethics approval beyond the project's existing
    approval for public data, and was not undertaken; we considered a
    single-investigator annotation pass and rejected it as too close to the
    system under evaluation to count as independent evidence. Every result in
    Sections~\ref{sec:baselines}--\ref{sec:conformal} is therefore agreement
    with an audited but imperfect extraction pipeline (Finding A), not verified
    accuracy. Section~\ref{sec:ood}'s OOD scores and
    Section~\ref{sec:interpretability}'s behavioral diagnostics are less
    directly affected, although two OOD setups and the \texttt{Play} check are
    defined using the regex's labels. Independent annotation, with whatever
    ethics review the authors' institution requires, is the highest-value
    extension of this work, since none of the modeling in
    Sections~\ref{sec:baselines}--\ref{sec:conformal} can currently be checked
    against anything but itself.
  \item \textbf{Sampling bias.} The corpus is conditioned on which
    channels operators chose to run, which videos they chose to title and
    describe in keyword-matchable ways, and YouTube's own recommendation
    and search surfacing, so it is not a random or exhaustive sample of
    ransomware-relevant discourse.
  \item \textbf{ASR noise.} 92.7\% of transcripts are machine-transcribed,
    mostly by \texttt{whisper\_base}. Section~\ref{sec:perturbation}'s
    simulated-ASR-noise sweep bounds how much this moves predictions;
    Finding B's word-error-rate-on-jargon question (comparing ASR output
    against source audio directly, rather than a simulation of ASR-style
    errors) remains open.
  \item \textbf{Weak-label ceiling.} Every quantitative result in
    Sections~\ref{sec:baselines}--\ref{sec:conformal} is bounded by the
    Knowledge Agent's own precision/recall, which Finding A shows is not
    perfect even after the disambiguation fix, and which
    Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual} shows leaving a weak trace in at
    least one trained model.
  \item \textbf{Source rights and platform terms.} The transcripts derive
    from talks, webinars, and walkthroughs whose speakers and channel
    operators hold their own rights, and they were obtained through the
    YouTube Data API, \texttt{yt-dlp}, and audio download for Whisper
    transcription. The release contains transcript text and metadata but
    no audio or video. A video being public does not by itself grant a right to
    redistribute its speech as text, and we did not obtain permission from
    individual channels or speakers. YouTube's terms of service may also
    restrict automated collection of this kind, so re-users should check
    the terms that apply to them. The authors' CC0 dedication covers only
    the authors' own contributions (the extracted features, labels, and
    compiled metadata). It grants no re-use rights in the transcript text
    or the underlying talks, which remain with their owners and are shared
    here for research use. A rights holder can have a transcript removed
    from future releases by opening an issue on the repository or by
    contacting the corresponding author.
  \item \textbf{Dual use.} This corpus covers ransomware tactics, tools, and
    case studies from public educational content. Every underlying video is
    already public and indexed by YouTube's search, and was produced by
    defenders (vendors, DFIR practitioners, conference speakers) for other
    defenders. RansKnow discloses no technique, tool, or tactic that is not
    already public. The MITRE ATT\&CK knowledge base and the CTI datasets in
    Section~\ref{sec:related} \citep{lim2017malwaretextdb, lange2024annoctr}
    already structure comparable information at finer granularity and higher
    label quality than RansKnow's weak-label extraction
    (\Cref{sec:gold-scope}). RansKnow adds a different modality (transcribed
    spoken narration rather than written reports) and a scale accessible
    without specialized CTI tooling. The realistic marginal risk is a small
    reduction in the effort needed to \emph{aggregate} already-scattered public
    information, the same tradeoff every public threat-intelligence aggregation
    resource makes. Given \Cref{sec:gold-scope}'s weak-label ceiling and
    Section~\ref{sec:baselines}'s weak family and tool classification, the
    corpus currently favors defensive research uses (weak-supervision
    methodology, corpus-level trend analysis) far more than offensive use, for
    which primary-source technical documentation would be a more direct and
    reliable starting point.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented RansKnow, an open MITRE ATT\&CK-aligned corpus of 1{,}128
ransomware-related YouTube video records, 1{,}034 with transcripts. The
audit shows that a plausible regex label can be dominated by detections a
simple context check rejects: 71\% of pre-fix \texttt{Play} detections
(\Cref{tab:alias-audit}). A corpus released without such an audit would
carry this error silently.

Against the audited labels, models reproduce relevance well (macro-F1
0.90) but dominant tactic poorly (0.38). In out-of-distribution detection,
softmax methods fail a sanity check that Mahalanobis distance passes, yet
Mahalanobis distance scores below chance when out-of-distribution videos
are selected for naming a family. The audit and the counterfactual and
perturbation checks can be reused on any dataset labeled by keyword or
regex extraction over scraped or transcribed text.

The priority for future work is independent human annotation of a subset
of the labels (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Until then, the baseline,
calibration, and conformal results describe agreement with the pipeline: a
macro-F1 of 0.90 on \texttt{relevance} means a LightGBM classifier on TF-IDF
reproduces the regex output at that rate, not that it identifies relevant
videos at that rate.
